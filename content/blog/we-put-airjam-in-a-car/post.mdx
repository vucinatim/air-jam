On the way to our coworker Žiga's bachelor party, we played Last Band Standing
on the screen in Domen's BMW. The passengers answered from their phones, the
music played through the car speakers, and the normal Air Jam flow worked much
like it would on a laptop or television.

We had fun with the quiz, but the bigger reaction was to the setup itself. The
people in the car already knew Air Jam and had played it before. Some of them
had even helped build Code Review and The Office during our zerodays game jam.
What they had not seen was Air Jam running in a BMW.

![Placeholder for the final cover photo showing Last Band Standing running on the BMW display](/blog-assets/we-put-airjam-in-a-car/cover-placeholder.svg)

## "Can Air Jam work in my car?"

It started the morning before the trip, when Domen sent me a message and a
screenshot from Google's Android Auto Desktop Head Unit.

He had been experimenting with a small Android app that could render Air Jam on
an Android Auto surface. By the time he messaged me, he had already solved the
difficult part. The Air Jam Arcade was visibly running inside Android
Auto, and the normal phone controller flow still worked.

![Domen's first proof of Air Jam rendering in the Android Auto Desktop Head Unit](/blog-assets/we-put-airjam-in-a-car/first-desktop-head-unit-proof.webp)

His request was simple: could I make the UI a little friendlier for the car,
perhaps with a larger layout or a URL parameter? He would find the screen
resolution and send it over.

The resolution arrived a few minutes later: `1920x720`, including the permanent
BMW sidebar. It is a very wide screen with not much height, plus vehicle
controls around the edges. Air Jam rendered, but that did not yet mean we could
comfortably play a complete game on it.

## We were not the first people to think of this

Domen had never played AirConsole. Most of the friends and coworkers on the
trip had never heard of it either. When he presented the idea, I told him that
AirConsole, the platform that originally inspired Air Jam, had already been
working with BMW on almost exactly this format for years.

[BMW and AirConsole announced their partnership in 2022](https://corp.airconsole.com/airconsole-bmw-partnership/).
In their official integration, games run directly in supported vehicle
infotainment systems and players use their phones as controllers.

The funniest part was that Domen's car was also a BMW.

We definitely had not invented in-car multiplayer gaming. What amused me was
that Domen had independently arrived at the same conclusion simply by looking
at Air Jam's architecture. If almost any shared browser-capable screen can
become the console, why should the screen in his car be any different?

AirConsole's automotive work is an official integration created with vehicle
manufacturers and supported infotainment systems. Ours was a private experiment
involving Domen's Android wrapper, a responsive web app, and the deadline: "We
leave tomorrow."

## Rendering was only the first proof

The car screen did not need to become a controller. That was an important
constraint from the beginning. The driver was not going to play, and the
passengers needed to operate everything from their phones.

That meant they had to be able to join the room, choose Last Band Standing,
select categories, ready up, start the match, answer every question, see the
results, restart, and return to the Arcade without touching the dashboard.

The host screen still had to do its part. It needed to remain readable inside a
very wide and shallow viewport. Rankings and timers could not disappear under
vehicle chrome. YouTube videos had to render through an Android app, a WebView,
a virtual display, and Android Auto. Audio then had to make it all the way to
the car speakers.

Domen had proved that the basic route was possible. Our job for the rest of the
day was to turn "it renders" into "we can trust it for a complete match
tomorrow."

![The Air Jam Arcade on Domen's wide BMW display during development, with Last Band Standing visible in the bottom right](/blog-assets/we-put-airjam-in-a-car/bmw-wide-screen-proof.webp)

## Making the normal product work better

I did not want to build a separate car edition of Air Jam. The car was one
unusual shared screen, not a reason to scatter Android Auto conditions
throughout the framework.

Instead, we made the existing experience respond more cleanly to the space it
actually had. We tested the Arcade and the game at the BMW's `1920x720`
resolution, then at smaller and less forgiving layouts down to `800x480`. We
kept game selection and the entire match lifecycle on the phones. We made the
on-screen controller launcher less intrusive during normal play, while
preserving it for first-time visitors who want to try Air Jam without
connecting another device.

The experiment also exposed things that were not car problems at all. They were
ordinary product problems that happened to become obvious on a strange screen.
Fixing them would make the normal browser experience better too.

Last Band Standing needed the most attention. Answer options could occasionally
repeat. Worse, a Slovenian song could appear beside three international songs,
which made the correct answer rather easy to spot even if you had never heard
the track. We changed the quiz so all four choices come from the same category
as the playing song and are always unique.

We also improved the controller results between rounds, made the final
standings scroll properly for larger groups, clarified category selection, and
cleaned up the host layout. Then we expanded and rebalanced the catalog. By the
end of the day it contained 206 songs across ten categories, including 59
Slovenian tracks and a much healthier selection of songs that were not obvious
within the first two seconds.

That work had very little to do with cars. The unusual deadline simply gave us
a good reason to finish it.

## Proving everything we could without the car

The car could not become our primary test environment. That would have made
every UI adjustment slow and every bug difficult to reproduce.

We treated the experience as a browser product first. We exercised the real
screen dimensions, the narrower usable area left by vehicle controls, multiple
phone sizes, and rooms with up to ten controllers. We played through the lobby,
countdown, active round, answer reveal, final standings, restart, and return
flows. We ran the Android wrapper against Google's Desktop Head Unit and a
physical phone connected over USB.

We each had an AI coding assistant in the loop. Domen worked with Claude on the
Android wrapper. I worked with Codex on the Air Jam platform, Last Band
Standing, validation, and release. On my side, that meant using the same
agent-facing development and testing contracts that Air Jam provides to game
developers. Domen still owned the proof that only his actual BMW could give us.

By late afternoon, almost every question was answered. The only meaningful
unknowns were whether the nested YouTube player would render in the real car
and whether its audio would reach the speakers.

Domen tested both. Video played. Sound played. We built a signed, versioned APK
that he could install from GitHub, while keeping the earlier version available
as a fallback. Last Band Standing went through Air Jam's normal hosted release
pipeline and appeared in the production Arcade.

At that point, it was ready for the trip.

## Then we actually played it

![Placeholder for a final photo of Last Band Standing running in the BMW during the road trip](/blog-assets/we-put-airjam-in-a-car/road-trip-result-placeholder.svg)

The next day, everyone joined from the phones they already had. The car
displayed the game, the music played through its speakers, and we guessed songs
on the way to celebrate our friend.

There was no dramatic production failure and no emergency rollback to the
backup APK. It just worked.

The group enjoyed Last Band Standing, but the thing that made the biggest
impression was still the setting. They already knew Air Jam, and some of them
had helped build games for it, but they had never played AirConsole or seen this
kind of game running in a car. Seeing a familiar project appear in a BMW was
still surprising.

I agreed with them.

## What the experiment proved

A car is not suddenly the next major platform on the Air Jam roadmap. This was
not official Android Auto support, and Domen's wrapper is not something we plan
to publish as a driving app. The passengers operated the game from their
phones. The driver did not play.

The useful result was much simpler. Air Jam is based on the idea that the
shared screen should be replaceable. A laptop, television, projector, office
display, or the unusually wide screen in a BMW can all host the same room. The
phones people already carry provide the controls.

Air Jam is mostly an after-work pet project, built by one developer. A lot of
the work happens in tests, architecture decisions, release scripts, and small
UI adjustments. The road trip was a chance to use it outside a game jam,
development session, or deliberate playtest. Everyone joined, played, and had
fun.

For a solo project, that kind of real use is motivating. It is one thing to
know that the architecture should work on different screens. It is another to
watch your friends use it in a BMW on a road trip.

Air Jam is open source at
[github.com/vucinatim/air-jam](https://github.com/vucinatim/air-jam), and the
[Air Jam Arcade](https://airjam.io/arcade) is available to try in a normal
browser. A BMW is entirely optional.
